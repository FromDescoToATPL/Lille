Practical Information for Visiting Lille

Everything that doesn't fit into the places/restaurants/bars entries, but makes a trip easier.

Getting to Lille

Two stations serve the city: Lille Europe for high-speed connections (Eurostar from London, Thalys from Brussels, TGV from Paris), and Lille Flandres for regional trains (TER) into Belgium and the rest of the Hauts-de-France region. The two stations face each other, a 5-minute walk apart.

Getting around once you're there

The city centre is very walkable — most places in this guide are within 20 minutes on foot of each other. For longer trips:

  • The metro (Ilévia network) has 2 fully automated, driverless lines — a curiosity in itself, it was one of the very first automated metro systems in the world
  • Tickets are sold at vending machines in every station (card payment accepted)
  • A day pass (around €5, unlimited rides) is better value than single tickets if you're making several trips in one day

Parking

On-street parking is chargeable from 9am to 7pm, Monday to Saturday. It's free from 7pm to 9am, and all day on Sundays and public holidays. One common misconception: unlike many French cities, Lille does not make parking free in August.

My tip, and it saves a few euros every visit: rather than circling the city centre where everything is chargeable, park over by the Citadelle. The regulated zones stop before the park, and you then walk into town across the moats and tree-lined paths — fifteen to twenty minutes on foot, through the city's largest green space instead of through traffic. It's a pleasant walk in spring and summer, and often quicker than you'd expect.

One word of caution: the paid perimeter has expanded into several neighbourhoods in recent years and keeps changing. Always check the signs and look for a ticket machine before leaving the car.

Paying while you're there

Card payment is accepted almost everywhere in France, even for small amounts. Still, carry a bit of cash for certain market stalls, a few traditional bars, or in case a card reader is down — rarer than elsewhere, but it happens. Cash machines are plentiful in the centre.

Safety and common sense

Lille is a pleasant and broadly safe city, but it's a large one: better to know where and when to pay more attention than to find out on the spot.

The station district and Euralille after dark. By day it's a busy business and shopping area with no particular difficulty. Once the shops close the mood shifts noticeably: the open spaces empty out and the area becomes far less welcoming. Nothing unusual for a European station district, but it isn't somewhere to linger late without reason. If you arrive by train at night, head straight to your accommodation.

Late-night bar closing time. Around rue Solférino and Masséna there's a lot of drinking, and weekend nights can get rowdy at closing. Not dangerous as such, but easily avoided by not hanging around once the bars shut.

Vieux-Lille stays lively until late and is the most comfortable area for an evening walk. Be aware, though, that the streets leading away from it empty out within a few hundred metres.

Pickpocketing is by far the most common risk. Markets, the metro at rush hour, crowded terraces, and above all the Braderie: keep your phone and wallet out of sight, and don't leave a bag hanging on the back of a chair.

The rest is common sense: stick to busy streets at night rather than deserted shortcuts. The metro runs late at weekends; once it stops, a ride-hailing car stays cheap for trips within the city.

A word on language

A "bonjour" and "merci" on arrival always go down well, but don't worry too much: most shopkeepers in the centre and younger generations manage fine in English, and a translation app covers the rest.
